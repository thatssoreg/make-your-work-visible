# Make Your Work Visible
## Student field guide

Reggie Leonard · SDS Career Services · UVA School of Data Science

Edition 2.0.0 · October 7, 2026

## You have several ways to begin.

You do not have to come up with an entirely new project. You can revisit an assignment, practice a task from a job description, explore a question, or explain work you have already done. Choose a starting point that fits your interests and the time you have.

### Revisit an assignment.
Perhaps you finished the required analysis but never investigated a surprising result. You might improve a chart, explain a choice in the README, ask for a code review, or add a question to your project notes. Check the course’s sharing rules before making the material public.

### Look for tasks in a job description.
Open the [SDS Job & Internship Opportunities Board](https://docs.google.com/spreadsheets/d/1ZvqS41CV-JMLrdSUb2KL5kzCaB09tv_dinSuUwv6WX4/edit?gid=0#gid=0) and look for verbs: investigate, compare, explain, define, evaluate, collaborate. A task can suggest what to practice even when you are not yet ready for the whole role.

Two possible examples are [Figma’s Data Science Intern (2027)](https://job-boards.greenhouse.io/figma/jobs/6178857004) and [CSIS’s Data Visualization Design Intern](https://job-boards.greenhouse.io/centerforstrategicandinternationalstudiesinc/jobs/4390739009). Figma’s application form was visible on October 7, 2026; CSIS was last checked October 6, 2026. The workshop’s suggested student activities are adaptations, not employer assignments. A posting can close; its task can still help you think about work you would like to learn.

Try one of these sentences:

- “I can already ___. To get closer to ___, I will practice ___ using ___.”
- “I want to understand ___. I will compare ___ and ___, then explain what I notice.”
- “I have not worked with ___ yet. My next step is ___, and I will document what I learn.”

A project can help you practice a task. It cannot manufacture employment experience, eligibility, or a credential you do not have. Also distinguish a skill you lack from one you have not yet demonstrated clearly.

### Borrow a dataset and ask your own question.
[TidyTuesday](https://github.com/rfordatascience/tidytuesday) offers recurring datasets and examples of how others explore them. [Data.gov](https://data.gov/) links to government datasets. [Kaggle Datasets](https://www.kaggle.com/datasets) offers datasets across many topics. Check the original source, coverage, license, and documentation before deciding what the data can tell you.

For a first attempt, write one question and make a small comparison. You do not need to enter a competition or reproduce a winning solution. Credit sources and distinguish your work from tutorials you followed.

### Get experience with data tools through a guided resource.
[DataLemur](https://datalemur.com/questions) organizes SQL and data interview questions by topic and difficulty; some material requires paid access. [SQLBolt](https://sqlbolt.com/) provides browser-based SQL lessons and exercises. Choose one exercise, work through the provider’s explanation, and record what you understand differently afterward. Do not repost restricted questions or solutions.

Use course materials, the provider’s explanation, or a technical colleague when you need help evaluating a solution.

### Practice GitHub with official instructions.
[GitHub Skills: Introduction to GitHub](https://github.com/skills/introduction-to-github) gives you a guided way to work with a branch, commit, and pull request. [GitHub’s Hello World guide](https://docs.github.com/en/get-started/start-your-journey/hello-world) is another browser-first introduction. A small documentation change can give you a reason to practice collaboration without inventing a large coding project.

### Begin a Handshake mission.
The [Create Your First Website and Share It on GitHub mission](https://joinhandshake.com/learn/create-your-first-website-and-share-it-on-github-b03d6708/) is listed at 25 minutes and uses v0 with a public GitHub repository. Your account may have additional access or setup requirements. Use only material you can publish. In your available work time, one saved step is a reasonable outcome; finishing the mission and earning a badge may take longer.

Find the current mission in your Handshake account. Follow its own instructions rather than treating the workshop as a substitute for them. [Handshake explains the mission workflow here](https://support.joinhandshake.com/hc/en-us/articles/43389166936599-How-do-I-complete-a-mission-in-AI-Skills-Studio).

### Find more examples before choosing a project.
Sometimes nothing resonates because you have not encountered enough of the work yet. Read a project write-up, attend a talk, visit a meetup, or ask someone about a problem they are working on. Record one problem, one unfamiliar concept, and one question. Those notes can become a starting point later.


## Choose projects that show relevant variety.

Start with what you want a reader to learn about you. Two analyses of different subjects may still demonstrate the same narrow set of skills. A thoughtful extension to one project can reveal more than another nearly identical notebook.

### Look beyond the tool list.

| Kind of work | What a reader might inspect | A way to begin |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Analysis and recommendations | A chart, a decision memo, and an explanation of uncertainty | Revisit a class analysis and explain what someone could do with the finding. |
| Relational databases or APIs | How sources relate, what you combined, and the question the result supports | Work through a provider-authored SQL exercise, then explore an original question with permitted data. |
| Cloud or deployed tools | A usable demo, an explanation of what you built, and its limits | Put a small existing project somewhere another person can use it. Check costs and access settings first. |
| Collaboration or open source | Your contribution, a discussion, a review, or a pull request | Improve documentation or contribute a change the project welcomes. |
| Research or evaluation | A comparison, replication attempt, or explanation of an unexpected result | Return to a question that the assignment did not require you to resolve. |
| Domain knowledge | A precise question grounded in a field and an explanation of context | Use something you care about, such as sport, public policy, education, or the environment. |

These are options, not a checklist. Relational data, cloud work, and collaboration can add relevant evidence, but no tool or project category guarantees that a candidate will stand out. A project should show choices you understand, not only a service you activated.

### How do you know the work is good?

Compare it with your course rubric, an established example, or the expectations in the work you want to do. Ask a faculty member, technical peer, or practitioner to examine a specific part. Ask whether the evidence supports the conclusion and whether someone else can follow your process. Then record the feedback and what you changed.

Reading other people's work, attending talks, and watching a project discussion also build a frame of reference. Ask what made an approach appropriate in its particular context rather than copying the tools it used.

[GitHub's profile guidance](https://docs.github.com/en/account-and-profile/tutorials/using-your-github-profile-to-enhance-your-resume) recommends selecting relevant projects that show varied skills, including contributions as well as work you own. The examples above are workshop suggestions, not a hiring formula.

### Contribute without making extra work for a maintainer.

Read the project's README, contribution guide, and open issues first. A useful documentation correction can be a contribution. For a substantial change, discuss it before doing the work, follow the project's conventions, and review any AI assistance carefully. A submitted pull request is not the same as an accepted contribution. See [GitHub's Open Source Guide](https://opensource.guide/how-to-contribute/).


## Explain your work clearly.

Whether you are revising a README, drafting a project description, or preparing an interview answer, start with the same questions. A reader should not need to infer your contribution from a list of software.

### 1. What problem were you trying to solve?
Describe the question, situation, or decision. Explain why it interested you or why it mattered to someone else. Give enough scope to make the question understandable.

### 2. How did you approach it?
Describe what you did technically and explain one consequential choice. Identify your contribution, especially in a group project. Methods and tools belong here when they help the reader understand the work, not simply because they are keywords.

### 3. What happened, and what does it mean?
Describe the result or current progress. Link to something a reader can inspect. Explain a limitation or an unanswered question. An unfinished project can have a clear account of what you tried and what remains unresolved.

An unfinished project can still have a useful explanation. Distinguish what you plan to do from what you have already attempted or observed.

### Another model: connect a decision to an analysis.

Reggie also uses a model learned from a colleague: turn a business question into a data question, obtain a data answer, and translate that answer back into a business answer. “Business” can also mean a community, research, or personal decision.

| Stage | Example of the reasoning |
| --- | --- |
| Business question | What should a team understand before deciding whether to keep a new feature? |
| Data question | Which behaviors would indicate that people find the feature useful, and what would we compare? |
| Data answer | Report only what the analysis actually finds. At the planning stage, this is not known yet. |
| Business answer | Explain what the finding supports, what remains uncertain, and what the team could reasonably do next. |

A data answer does not automatically establish a cause or a recommendation. The translation is where you make your assumptions and judgment visible.

### Turn the explanation into a conversation.

Ask a reader to look at your paragraph without talking them through it first. Then ask: “What do you understand? What would you like to know more about?” That is useful feedback for a README and practice for explaining a project in an interview.

These are workshop communication models, not a technical assessment. Use [GitHub’s README guidance](https://docs.github.com/en/repositories/managing-your-repositorys-settings-and-features/customizing-your-repository/about-readmes) for the documentation needs of a repository, and ask an instructor or technical peer to evaluate technical choices when appropriate.


## Choose the GitHub features that serve your work.

A repository holds a project and its history. A portfolio is a selection of work with enough context to understand it. GitHub can help present that selection, but a polished profile or separate website is not a prerequisite for starting.

### A profile README is an option for introducing yourself.

GitHub displays a profile README when a public repository matches your username and contains a nonempty `README.md` at its root. Follow [GitHub’s exact profile README instructions](https://docs.github.com/en/account-and-profile/how-tos/profile-customization/managing-your-profile-readme), including any account restrictions.

1. Open your own profile and note your exact username.
2. Check whether a repository with that same name already exists. If it does, edit its README instead of creating a duplicate.
3. Otherwise, follow GitHub’s instructions to create the public, username-matching repository and initialize its README.
4. Write an introduction and explain one piece of work you are allowed to share.
5. Preview the formatting, commit the change, and open your profile. Check project links as the intended reader would see them.

Compare [Ckalib S. Nelson’s profile](https://github.com/ckalibsnelson) and [Reggie Leonard’s profile](https://github.com/thatssoreg). Ckalib organizes professional work, community involvement, education, and current interests. Reggie uses project visuals and descriptions across several fields. Reggie took inspiration from Ckalib’s use of the feature, then made different design choices. Neither is a required template. Contribution counters, badges, and streaks are decorative choices, not a measure of your whole capability.

### A project README explains the project, not your whole career.

Start with the problem or question, your approach and contribution, and the results or current progress. Link to the chart, report, notebook, demonstration, or other evidence that supports the explanation. Include dependencies and exact run instructions when someone needs them.

GitHub’s [README guidance](https://docs.github.com/en/repositories/managing-your-repositorys-settings-and-features/customizing-your-repository/about-readmes) covers what a project does, why it is useful, how to get started, where to get help, and who maintains or contributes to it. The workshop’s three-question framework is a career-communication adaptation, not an exact GitHub template.

### Use enough structure to help a reader find the work.

The compact example below is **adapted** from [Cookiecutter Data Science’s directory conventions](https://cookiecutter-data-science.drivendata.org/) and [GitHub’s project-presentation guidance](https://docs.github.com/en/account-and-profile/tutorials/using-your-github-profile-to-enhance-your-resume). It is one example, not a required structure. Use only the folders your project needs, and follow any course or team conventions that apply.

```text
my-project/
  README.md       # Explains the project and how to explore it.
  notebooks/      # Holds exploratory notebooks, when relevant.
  src/            # Holds reusable project code, when relevant.
  reports/        # Holds an explanation, figures, or a report.
```

A small reproducible project is easier to follow than an elaborate empty structure. For a larger analysis, inspect [Cookiecutter Data Science](https://cookiecutter-data-science.drivendata.org/) as a reference and discuss the design with a technical instructor or teammate. The full template has additional directories and tooling that a small project may not need.

### A branch and pull request can help you practice collaboration.

For a repository you own or may contribute to, follow [GitHub’s browser-based Hello World guide](https://docs.github.com/en/get-started/start-your-journey/hello-world). Agree on a small change, make it on a branch, describe the change in a pull request, and ask someone to review it. A README improvement is a legitimate way to learn the workflow. Respect collaborators’ permissions and avoid changing other people’s work without agreement.

### Optional / extra credit: a separate website.

A website gives you more control over how selected work is presented. [GitHub Pages](https://docs.github.com/en/pages/getting-started-with-github-pages/what-is-github-pages) is a static hosting option, separate from a profile README. The availability of private-source publishing depends on your GitHub plan. The published website can be public even when the source repository is private. Check its visibility before adding information.


### A quick profile review

Can a visitor find a short introduction, selected projects, and a way to contact you? Do project titles and descriptions say what the work is about? Have you credited team contributions and made the next click obvious?

The short profile bio and optional longer profile README do different jobs. The README can link to LinkedIn and a public-safe résumé as well as to project repositories. GitHub's official [profile guidance](https://docs.github.com/en/account-and-profile/tutorials/using-your-github-profile-to-enhance-your-resume) is a useful starting point, not a requirement to make your page look one particular way.


## Choose a place that suits the work.

You do not need a separate website to begin. First decide what you want someone to find: a short introduction, selected projects, and a way to reach you. The platform should make those things easy to update and explore.

| Option | What it is useful for | What to consider |
| --- | --- | --- |
| GitHub profile README | A flexible introduction and links to selected work, displayed on your GitHub profile | It is a public README in a repository matching your username. It is not the same feature as GitHub Pages. |
| GitHub Pages | A separate static website built from files in a repository | Public-repository hosting is available with GitHub Free. Private-source options depend on your plan. You maintain the site files and links. |
| Wix | A visual site builder with portfolio templates and hosting | Free publishing is available; a custom domain or removal of Wix ads needs an upgrade. |
| Squarespace | A visual website with project pages and an organized portfolio landing page | Full public publication requires a paid site. A trial is private by default, with password-sharing options. |
| A site you build yourself | A custom experience that can itself demonstrate your design and development decisions | Decide where it will be hosted and how you will maintain it. Check provider pricing, security, and any paid APIs before publishing. |

Official references: [GitHub profile README](https://docs.github.com/en/account-and-profile/how-tos/profile-customization/managing-your-profile-readme), [GitHub Pages](https://docs.github.com/en/pages/getting-started-with-github-pages/what-is-github-pages), [Wix portfolios](https://www.wix.com/portfolio-website), [Squarespace portfolio pages](https://support.squarespace.com/hc/en-us/articles/360035611791-Portfolio-pages), and [Squarespace site visibility](https://support.squarespace.com/hc/en-us/articles/206536837-Is-my-site-live). These platform details were checked October 7, 2026; review current terms before paying or publishing.

### Content to plan before choosing a template

**About you.** Write a short introduction to your studies, interests, and the work you are exploring. You can include selected experience or relevant skills, but give the reader examples rather than an unconnected list.

**Selected projects.** Give each project a clear title, a short explanation, and a link to the work. A chart, report, screenshot, demonstration, or permitted case description may be more useful than sending everyone to a repository root.

**Contact and background.** Link to LinkedIn, a professional contact channel, and a résumé when it helps. Prepare a public-safe résumé without a home address or other details you do not want broadly available.

**Additional sections.** Writing, collaboration, research, talks, and work in progress can be useful when you have material for them. You do not need empty sections waiting for future accomplishments.

### Before publishing

Open the page while signed out. Check that project links work, text is readable on a phone, and public material does not expose private course, employer, or collaborator content. A private repository link will not become visible just because you put it on a public page.

Selling software or services is a separate decision from using a website as a portfolio. You can publish a learning project without turning it into a business.


## Decide what you are allowed to share.


**Coursework:** Read the syllabus and assignment rules. Ask the instructor when the answer is not clear. Do not expose solutions that classmates or future students are expected to produce independently.

**Internships and employment:** Obtain permission for the specific material, including charts, descriptions, data, screenshots, and code. Changing a company name or hiding source code does not by itself make a case study shareable.

**Group projects:** Describe your own contribution and credit others. Check that collaborators agree to the publication and that you may redistribute every input.

**Sensitive material:** Keep personal information and credentials out of public files. If a credential has been exposed, removing a visible file does not necessarily remove its history. Follow [GitHub’s guidance on sensitive data](https://docs.github.com/en/authentication/keeping-your-account-and-data-secure/removing-sensitive-data-from-a-repository) and revoke or rotate the exposed credential.

A private explanation is a useful workshop output. Publishing can happen after the permission question has been resolved.


## Introduce your work with enough context to invite a response.

You do not have to send someone your whole portfolio. Choose a project, question, or finding related to the conversation and explain why you thought it might interest them.

### Before a confirmed informational interview

“Thank you for making time to speak with me. I have been working on a project about how people start using a new product, and I thought it might provide context for my questions about product analytics. There is no need to read it beforehand, but here is a short project description: [link].”

Include the real meeting context, a brief description, and an optional link. Do not assign the other person homework.

### Reaching out because of a shared interest

“I noticed that you work in sports analytics. I am a data science student exploring questions about player performance, and I was excited to find someone doing related work professionally. Would you be open to a brief conversation about what your work looks like day to day?”

Name a genuine connection, describe your work accurately, and make a considerate request. Add a project link when it supplies useful context rather than a demand to review your whole portfolio.

### A short project post

Explain what prompted the question, one decision or finding, and what you learned or would investigate next. Include a useful visual or link. Be clear about whether the work is planned, in progress, or complete.

For example: “After a class project, I realized that my chart needed more context for someone who had not worked with the data. I revised the title and caption, then asked a classmate to explain what they took from it. The comparison helped me see where my original explanation relied on assumptions. Here are the two versions and what I changed: [link].”

These are writing examples. Change the details to match work and conversations that actually happened.

### A technical write-up

The Artifact writing kit offers structures for explaining a system, presenting an analysis, teaching a concept, reporting a research attempt, or building a visual narrative. Choose a structure that fits your reader and evidence; it is not necessary to use every section.


### LinkedIn and other places to share

A project post, a permitted project link in an application, or a link before an interview can each serve a different purpose. LinkedIn's [Featured section](https://www.linkedin.com/help/linkedin/answer/a550399) is another place to make selected work easier to find. Check how the link appears to another person, including when they are signed out.

### An optional partner check

Show someone your draft without explaining it first. Ask what they understand about the project and what is still unclear. Revise one sentence. A private draft can be enough; you do not need to send or publish it in the workshop.


## Use AI to make progress while checking your understanding.

A coach can help you scope a question. A critic can identify missing information in a README. A coding assistant can propose a change. A pulse checker can ask you to explain a decision. These are different uses of a tool; completing a task does not by itself establish that you understand it.

Before accepting a change, ask what changed, why, and what comes next. Test what can be tested and explain the relevant reasoning in your own words. Follow your course’s AI rules and the permissions that apply to any data, code, or private material you provide to a service.

## Work options

### Write or improve your About section

Introduce your interests and the kind of work you are learning to do, then make relevant projects easy to find.

**Where:** Your GitHub bio, profile README, LinkedIn About section, or a document.

1. Write two or three sentences about what you study, the questions you care about, and the work you are exploring.
2. Choose one or two projects that make those interests concrete. Add direct links when you can share them.
3. Add a professional way to contact you, your LinkedIn profile, and a public-safe résumé if useful. Ask a neighbor what they now understand about you.

**Finish line:** You have an introduction and a short list of links that support it.

**Fallback:** Use the profile outline and leave space for project links you will add later.

### Create or revise your profile README

Introduce your interests and selected work in a way that reflects you. A plain-text introduction is a valid starting point.

**Where:** Your username-matching repository, following GitHub’s official steps.

1. Compare Reggie’s and Ckalib’s profiles. Choose one organizational idea you would use differently.
2. Follow the official profile README instructions. If the repository already exists, edit it rather than creating another.
3. Write a short introduction and explain one project you may share. Check that the project link works for the intended reader.

**Finish line:** Your profile has a clearer introduction or one more useful project description.

**Fallback:** Draft the introduction locally while you resolve account access later.

### Explain a project in your README

Use the three-question framework to help someone understand work you have already done.

**Where:** Your existing project README, or a document you can paste into it later.

1. Choose a project and write the problem or question in language a classmate outside the course could understand.
2. Describe your technical approach and your contribution. Explain one decision instead of listing every tool.
3. Add the result or current progress, a link to evidence, and one limitation. Ask a partner what is still unclear.

**Finish line:** You have a revised opening and know which evidence it should link to.

**Fallback:** Work from an assignment description and draft only the problem and your actual contribution.

### Extend an assignment you already finished

Return to a part you rushed or a question the assignment did not require you to explore.

**Where:** The original notebook, project, chart, or a short planning document.

1. Identify one specific change: a clearer chart, a more careful explanation, a code review request, or a question you left open.
2. Make a change you understand and can explain. For technical uncertainty, use your course resources or ask a technical peer.
3. Record what changed and why. If implementation needs more time, save an issue that describes the next action.

**Finish line:** You have a small revision or a well-defined next step, with a reason for it.

**Fallback:** Write the question and the first thing you need to inspect rather than starting a large rebuild.

### Explain work you cannot publish in full

Find out what you may share before writing a public project description.

**Where:** A private note until permission is clear.

1. Check the assignment, internship, employer, data, and collaborator restrictions.
2. Draft only the problem, your role, and an approach or lesson you are permitted to discuss. Leave out identifying or proprietary details.
3. List any approval still needed. Ask the relevant person before publishing.

**Finish line:** You have a permitted explanation or a clear approval request.

**Fallback:** Work with a separate public-data example, or keep the note private.

### Borrow a dataset and find a question

Explore an existing source without treating every available column as a project requirement.

**Where:** A dataset page, spreadsheet, notebook, or your notes.

1. Open Data.gov, Kaggle, TidyTuesday, or another source relevant to your interests.
2. Inspect the description and a few fields. Write one question the data might help answer, and one they probably cannot.
3. Save the source link, access conditions, and a first inspection you can realistically make.

**Finish line:** You have a specific question tied to a source you have actually inspected.

**Fallback:** Read a project in the collection and note the question you would ask differently.

### Build a bridge from a job description

Use a task in a role you want to identify something worthwhile to practice next.

**Where:** The SDS board or one of the employer examples, plus a note.

1. Open a posting and identify a task expressed as an action, not only a skill or tool name.
2. Write what you can already demonstrate and what you would like to practice. Use the bridge examples for scale.
3. Choose a small investigation, comparison, explanation, or project extension. Record the first action and what you hope to learn.

**Finish line:** You have a realistic next experience connected to a task that interests you.

**Fallback:** Use the Figma or CSIS task excerpt saved in the workshop if the live posting closes or access fails.

### Practice with an external learning resource

Use a provider-authored exercise to investigate the learning gap you identified.

**Where:** DataLemur, SQLBolt, or GitHub Skills.

1. Pick one question or lesson at a level you can reasonably attempt today. Check whether it requires an account or paid access.
2. Work in the resource’s own interface. Use its explanations and your course resources when you need technical help.
3. Write a brief note about your approach, an unfamiliar concept, or where you got stuck. Credit the resource.

**Finish line:** You attempted a relevant step and can name what you learned or where you need help.

**Fallback:** Read the question and outline your approach without needing to finish a technical solution.

### Start a Handshake mission

Follow Handshake’s guided activity using access that is already available to you.

**Where:** Handshake AI Skills Studio in your own account.

## Full coaching prompts

### Fluency

Help me develop a small project that I will understand well enough to explain and extend.

Start by asking for: an assignment or question that interests me; what I have already done; what I know how to do; the time I can realistically spend; and the data or tools I can access. Ask no more than three questions at a time.

Offer three possible next steps, not three full applications. At least one should extend work I already have. For each, explain the question, the first action, what I might learn, and what would be enough for this round. Do not assume I want to build software. A comparison, explanation, interview, or investigation may be more useful.

After I choose, help me work through one step at a time. Ask me to predict what I expect before I run an analysis. Explain unfamiliar terms in the context of my task. When suggesting code, explain what it changes and how I could check it. Do not claim you ran anything unless you actually did, and distinguish my observations from your suggestions.

At the end, ask me: What changed? Why? What would I try next? Help me document what I can now explain, along with questions I still have. Do not turn plans into completed accomplishments.

### Legibility

Help me explain one project using the same three questions throughout:
1. What problem or question was I working on?
2. How did I approach it technically, and what was my contribution?
3. What happened, what evidence supports it, and what remains uncertain?

Before drafting, ask for my notes or existing README, the intended reader, whether this is individual or group work, and the actual status. Use only material I am allowed to share. Ask what is missing before you rewrite anything.

Review the existing explanation against those three questions. Separate missing information from weak writing. Do not invent numbers, results, business impact, tests, or responsibilities. Keep my individual contribution distinct from the team's work.

Draft an opening of roughly 120–180 words in complete, natural sentences. Preserve my voice. Include relevant tools where they explain the approach, not as an unexplained keyword list. If the project is unfinished, say what I have done and what I will examine next.

Then suggest where a result, figure, code file, or setup instruction should be linked. Use placeholders for evidence I have not supplied. Ask me to check the accuracy and explain one important decision without relying on the draft.

### Reach

Help me share one project in a way that is relevant to another person.

Ask for the actual project or question, my contribution and its current status, the person or audience, and why the connection is genuine. Find out whether this is a public post, an initial outreach note, a confirmed informational interview, or a technical write-up. Do not assume a conversation has already happened.

For a short post, help me explain what interested me, what I tried, what I learned or still wonder about, and where someone can see the work.
For an initial note, connect a specific part of the person's publicly described work to a genuine interest or project of mine, then make one reasonable request.
For a confirmed conversation, thank them and offer an optional project link as context, explicitly without giving them homework.
For a technical write-up, outline the problem, approach, a decision or difficulty, evidence, and what I learned.

Write complete sentences, avoid marketing language and inflated claims, and do not say that public writing guarantees interviews. Do not invent familiarity or flatter someone based on unsupported information. Preserve the difference between an idea, a prototype, and a finished result. Give me one draft and two questions to help me improve it. Do not post or send anything.

### Bridge

Help me identify a small project that connects what I can do now with work I would like to do.

I will provide a job or internship description (with its URL and date), examples of my current work, my interests, and my available time. Do not evaluate my overall employability or give me a match score.

First, extract up to three tasks from the description. Quote the task accurately and separate it from tool names, credentials, and years of experience. Do not assume that every listed requirement is essential or that the employer will accept a project in place of a qualification.

For each task, ask: What can I already demonstrate? What have I not yet had the chance to practice or explain? What would a small, realistic next experience look like?

Suggest a few alternatives: extending an assignment, comparing two approaches, practicing with an external exercise, using a public dataset, writing an explanation for a nontechnical reader, or discussing the work with a practitioner. Avoid assuming that every option must be an app or require proprietary data.

Separate the employer's actual task from your proposed learning activity. Identify a first step that fits the time I have today, any access restrictions, a possible output or insight, and a way to learn whether I am making progress. Be honest when data availability is unknown.

Help me finish one of these statements:
- I can already ___. To get closer to ___, I will practice ___ using ___.
- I want to understand ___. I will compare ___ and ___, then explain what I notice.
- I have not worked with ___ yet. My next step is ___, and I will document what I learn.

Ask me to choose before expanding the plan.

### Pulse

Help me check my understanding of my own work. I will provide a permitted project explanation or code excerpt. Ask one question at a time about the problem, my decisions, what changed, why, and what comes next. Ask me to predict what would happen under a small change before giving the answer. Do not grade my employability or equate confident language with understanding. If I cannot explain a decision, offer a hint and help me identify documentation, a course resource, or a technical person who could help. End with the gaps I identified, not a fabricated mastery score.

### Coach

I am working on [my selected project, assignment, job task, or question]. My current experience is [what I have actually done]. I have [time] and access to [permitted materials]. Help me choose a manageable next learning step. Ask one question at a time. Do not propose a complete project until you understand my question and constraints. Start by asking what I want to understand. Distinguish missing evidence from missing ability. Offer a hint before a worked example, and then ask me to make the next decision. Do not invent results, access to employer data, or a claim that the project qualifies me for a role.

### Critic

Read the project explanation I provide. Do not rewrite it yet. Tell me what a reader can and cannot establish under the same three questions: (1) What problem or question? (2) What approach and personal contribution? (3) What result or current progress, and what does it mean? Quote the relevant sentence when you identify a gap. Ask for missing facts instead of supplying them. Label plans as plans. When I answer, help me revise using only that evidence and keep my voice.

### Coding

I am trying to [bounded task] in my own project. Here is the relevant code, error, and what I expected: [only material I am allowed to share]. Help me investigate before replacing the code. Ask what I tried and explain unfamiliar concepts. Propose the smallest relevant change and a way to check it using the applicable documentation. State assumptions and do not claim a test ran unless it did. Then ask me to explain: What changed? Why? What should I try next? Pause for my answer.

## Artifact: writing outlines

### Builder: Explain how the system works

Help a reader follow the structure, a consequential choice, and what you checked.

- **Problem and constraints:** What were you trying to make possible, and what limited the approach? The reader needs to understand the task before the tool choices.
- **Structure:** Describe the main parts; add a diagram when it clarifies their relationship. A reader can see how inputs become outputs.
- **A technical decision:** Compare one choice with an alternative you considered. This gives the reader evidence of reasoning rather than a tool list.
- **Implementation:** Link to the relevant code or configuration and explain your contribution. The explanation points to something a reader can inspect.
- **A problem and a check:** Describe an actual obstacle and how you investigated it. Small debugging stories are useful when they are specific and accurate.
- **Limits and next steps:** Explain what remains untested or unfinished. A reader can distinguish a working part from a future plan.

### Analyst: Explain an analysis and its implications

Connect the question, evidence, and interpretation without inventing business impact.

- **Question:** What decision or uncertainty motivated the analysis? It establishes why the work matters.
- **Scope and data:** Describe the source, unit of analysis, period, and exclusions. The reader can see what the analysis includes.
- **Approach:** Explain the methods that matter to the question. Methods are understandable in context.
- **Findings:** Report what you actually observed, with a figure or result when available. A claim can be connected to evidence.
- **Meaning:** Explain what the result supports and what it does not. An observation is not automatically a causal answer.
- **Next decision:** Propose a next action, information need, or follow-up analysis. Separate recommendations and estimates from measured outcomes.

### Expert: Explain a concept in its domain

Make the knowledge accessible while being clear about prerequisites and limits.

- **Reader and prerequisites:** Who is the explanation for, and what should they already know? The starting assumptions become explicit.
- **Concept and relevance:** Explain the concept and why it matters in this setting. Domain context gives terminology meaning.
- **A concrete example:** Work through a small example in a way a reader can follow. Examples connect abstraction with use.
- **Your reasoning:** Explain the choices in the example, including technical details when needed. The reader can follow why the steps were chosen.
- **Limits:** Describe where the example stops or the explanation needs qualification. A small example is not a universal rule.
- **Sources and next learning:** Link to the original documentation or research you used. Readers can check and extend the explanation.

### Researcher: Document an investigation or reproduction

Make assumptions, methods, and results traceable, including inconclusive outcomes.

- **Question or claim:** Name the question you investigate or the published claim you examine. The scope of inquiry is clear.
- **Setup:** Document the data, procedure, and relevant conditions. Someone can understand what was actually attempted.
- **Unspecified choices:** Record gaps in the instructions and how you resolved them. Assumptions become visible rather than hidden.
- **Results:** Compare actual observations with the reference on comparable terms. A difference is described without inventing its cause.
- **Interpretation:** Discuss uncertainty and plausible explanations that remain to be tested. Inconclusive and negative results still deserve careful explanation.
- **Evidence:** Link to the paper, code version, logs, or records that support the account. The report has a traceable basis.

### Storyteller: Write a data story someone can follow

Guide the reader through a real question and the evidence that informs it.

- **Opening question:** Introduce an observation, question, or documented finding. The reader knows why the story exists.
- **Context:** Explain the setting and where the data came from. The evidence is not detached from its source.
- **Visual explanation:** Choose figures that each do a particular explanatory job. A chart should answer something, not simply decorate the page.
- **Interpretation:** Write what each figure supports and where uncertainty remains. Captions and text help the reader avoid overreading the image.
- **Main takeaway:** State the conclusion at the scale the evidence supports. The story does not need a dramatic claim to be useful.
- **What follows:** Suggest a question, implication, or further source. A reader knows what to think about next.
