## Explain your work clearly.

Whether you are revising a README, drafting a project description, or preparing an interview answer, start with the same questions. A reader should not need to infer your contribution from a list of software.

### 1. What problem were you trying to solve?
Describe the question, situation, or decision. Explain why it interested you or why it mattered to someone else. Give enough scope to make the question understandable.

### 2. How did you approach it?
Describe what you did technically and explain one consequential choice. Identify your contribution, especially in a group project. Methods and tools belong here when they help the reader understand the work, not simply because they are keywords.

### 3. What happened, and what does it mean?
Describe the result or current progress. Link to something a reader can inspect. Explain a limitation or an unanswered question. An unfinished project can have a clear account of what you tried and what remains unresolved.

An unfinished project can still have a useful explanation. Distinguish what you plan to do from what you have already attempted or observed.

### Another model: connect a decision to an analysis.

Reggie also uses a model learned from a colleague: turn a business question into a data question, obtain a data answer, and translate that answer back into a business answer. “Business” can also mean a community, research, or personal decision.

| Stage | Example of the reasoning |
| --- | --- |
| Business question | What should a team understand before deciding whether to keep a new feature? |
| Data question | Which behaviors would indicate that people find the feature useful, and what would we compare? |
| Data answer | Report only what the analysis actually finds. At the planning stage, this is not known yet. |
| Business answer | Explain what the finding supports, what remains uncertain, and what the team could reasonably do next. |

A data answer does not automatically establish a cause or a recommendation. The translation is where you make your assumptions and judgment visible.

### Turn the explanation into a conversation.

Ask a reader to look at your paragraph without talking them through it first. Then ask: “What do you understand? What would you like to know more about?” That is useful feedback for a README and practice for explaining a project in an interview.

These are workshop communication models, not a technical assessment. Use [GitHub’s README guidance](https://docs.github.com/en/repositories/managing-your-repositorys-settings-and-features/customizing-your-repository/about-readmes) for the documentation needs of a repository, and ask an instructor or technical peer to evaluate technical choices when appropriate.
